1. What the sales forecast does

The Sales Analytics forecast projects future store revenue and units sold from recent completed POS transactions. It is a deterministic trend-based heuristic. It does not train a machine-learning model.

Correct description for your defense: “The system uses a calendar-day sales baseline adjusted by a capped recent trend. It then generates higher and lower scenarios using historical volatility, momentum, and data coverage.”

Data included

  • Only sales whose payment status is completed, paid, successful, or success.
  • Cancelled, canceled, and fully returned transactions are excluded.
  • Revenue comes from the completed sale amount and line subtotals.
  • Units come from the quantity in each sale detail.
  • Dates are grouped into daily, weekly, monthly, quarterly, or annual buckets.

2. Algorithm pipeline

Completed POS sales | v Group revenue and quantity by calendar day | v Build up to 90 daily rows, including zero-sales days | +--> Average daily revenue and units +--> First-half vs second-half trend +--> Recent momentum and volatility +--> Selected-period trend and momentum | v Base forecast for each future period | +--> Steady projection +--> Higher scenario +--> Lower scenario +--> Directional probabilities +--> History-based confidence score

Why zero-sales days are included

The daily baseline includes every calendar day from the start of the model window to today. This prevents the average from being calculated only from busy days. A closed or inactive day therefore lowers the baseline, as it should for a calendar-based store forecast.

3. Exact calculation

3.1 Daily baseline

The model uses up to the last 90 calendar days, beginning later if the first recorded sale is more recent.

Average Daily Revenue = Σ Daily Revenue / Number of Calendar Days
Average Daily Units = Σ Daily Units / Number of Calendar Days

3.2 Daily trend

The daily rows are split in half. Their average revenues are compared, then the result is limited to ±25% so a short spike cannot create unlimited growth.

Raw Trend = (Second-Half Average - First-Half Average) / First-Half Average
Daily Trend = clamp(Raw Trend, -0.25, +0.25)

If the first-half average is zero, Daily Trend is set to zero.

3.3 Future-period multiplier

For future period h, where h begins at 1:

Multiplier[h] = max(0.10, 1 + Daily Trend × (h / 2))

The 0.10 floor prevents the forecast multiplier from becoming negative or zero during a continuing decline.

3.4 Revenue and unit forecast

Revenue Forecast[h] = round(Average Daily Revenue × Days in Period × Multiplier[h])
Unit Forecast[h] = round(Average Daily Units × Days in Period × Multiplier[h])

For a daily forecast, Days in Period = 1. A weekly forecast normally uses seven days, while monthly and quarterly forecasts use their actual calendar lengths.

4. Higher and lower scenarios

4.1 Period trend

The selected-period revenue buckets are split into halves and compared. The result is capped at ±35%.

Period Trend = clamp((Recent-Half Average - Earlier-Half Average) / Earlier-Half Average, -0.35, +0.35)

4.2 Scenario spread

Spread = clamp(|Period Trend| + 0.15, 0.12, 0.45)

When history is insufficient, Spread = 0.35.

Higher Scenario = round(Base Forecast × (1 + Spread))
Lower Scenario = max(0, round(Base Forecast × (1 - Spread)))

What the scenarios mean

They are sensitivity bands around the base forecast. They are not statistically calibrated prediction intervals. Use “higher case” and “lower case,” not “guaranteed maximum” and “guaranteed minimum.”

5. Probability and confidence

5.1 Momentum

The algorithm compares up to three recent selected-period buckets with the previous group of the same size. Momentum is capped at ±35%.

Signal = 0.60 × Period Trend + 0.40 × Period Momentum

5.2 Volatility

Variance = Σ(Revenue - Average Revenue)² / n
Volatility = min(1, Standard Deviation / Average Revenue)

5.3 History quality and directional probability

History Quality = min(1, max(Active Revenue Days / 12, Active Revenue Periods / 4))
Probability Confidence = History Quality × max(0.35, 1 - 0.35 × Volatility)
Horizon Decay[h] = max(0.45, 1 - 0.08 × h)
Higher Probability[h] = clamp(50 + round(Signal × 140 × Probability Confidence × Horizon Decay[h]), 20, 80)
Lower Probability[h] = 100 - Higher Probability[h]

5.4 Displayed confidence score

History is considered reliable when there are at least three active daily rows or at least two nonempty selected-period buckets.

Confidence = min(90, max(55, 52 + 7 × Number of Forecast-Base Periods))

If history is insufficient, confidence is fixed at 35%.

Important: this confidence value measures data coverage. It is not a statistically computed probability that the forecast will be correct.

7. What you must explain honestly

Strengths

  • Uses completed transaction data directly.
  • Includes zero-sales calendar days.
  • Caps extreme growth and decline signals.
  • Supports multiple reporting horizons with actual calendar lengths.
  • Transparent and inexpensive to compute.

Limitations

  • No explicit seasonality for Christmas, school opening, payday, weather, or holidays.
  • No causal variables such as promotions, price changes, or stockouts.
  • The multiplier compounds the same capped trend across the horizon and may become aggressive.
  • The higher/lower spread is heuristic, not learned from past forecast errors.
  • The probability and confidence labels are heuristic scores.
  • New stores and sparse data produce unstable forecasts.

Recommended upgrades

  1. Store every forecast with its generation date and later actual result.
  2. Measure MAE, RMSE, WAPE, and bias using rolling backtests.
  3. Calculate scenario intervals from historical residuals.
  4. Add weekday and seasonal baselines.
  5. Mark stockout days so constrained sales are not mistaken for low demand.
  6. Model promotional sales separately from normal baseline demand.
  7. Compare the existing heuristic against naive and seasonal-naive baselines.

8. Questions to prepare for

What algorithm does your Sales Forecast use?
A calendar-day average forecast adjusted by a capped first-half versus second-half sales trend, with volatility- and momentum-based higher and lower scenarios.

Is it machine learning?
No. It is a deterministic time-series heuristic. Its advantage is transparency; its limitation is that it does not learn seasonal or causal patterns.

Why cap the trend?
To stop a short sales spike or decline from creating unrealistic future values. Daily trend is capped at ±25%, while selected-period trend and momentum are capped at ±35%.

Why include zero-sales days?
The model forecasts calendar-period performance, so days with no completed sales must affect the average.

What does 80% higher probability mean?
It is a directional heuristic from trend, momentum, volatility, history quality, and forecast horizon. It is not an empirically calibrated statistical probability.

How would you prove forecast quality?
Save forecasts before the predicted period, compare them with later actual values, and report MAE, WAPE, RMSE, and bias against simple baselines.
